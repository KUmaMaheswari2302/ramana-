Publisher’s Note

From the time he started living on the mountain, Bhagavan Sri Ramana 
moved closely with monkeys. To his observant eyes, it became evident that 

they had their own culture, territories, forms of government and codes of war. 
Bhagavan watched them at sport and play, and bestowed his tender care on those 
in need. In time, they accepted him as one of their own. As with St. Francis of 
Assissi, Bhagavan had a special ability to communicate with animals that was 
beyond ordinary human understanding. 

Taken from chronicles by devotees, the anecdotes of the present volume come 
from Ashram publications, though some are appearing in print for the first time. 
Through the medium of stories, we hear about life on the Hill and of monkeys 
such as Bhagavan’s friend, Nondi. It is hard to believe that such things could 
have ever actually happened. Nevertheless, the events portrayed here are taken 
from the direct testimonies of devotees who witnessed them. Though segments 
of the story have been dramatised where there are gaps in the record, the sto-
ryline is nevertheless true to history. While the four divisions of the book follow 
the chronology of Bhagavan’s life, the chapters within each overlap and may 



not always follow a precise historical time sequence. Portions from the Ashram 
literature have been freely adapted throughout the text, paraphrased in some 
places and cited word for word in others.

Let us ponder the lives of these blessed souls who by Bhagavan’s grace were 
fortunate to be in his continuous presence and receive his blessings in full. May 
we not only be entertained and edified, but allow the compassion that Bhaga-
van so glowingly exemplified inform our interactions and relationships with all 
creatures — great and small. 

Karthigai Deepam Day V. S. Ramanan

In the Beginning 

Once upon a time, Lord Siva appeared on the earth in 
the form of a mountain. It wasn’t really his idea — the other Gods 
put him up to it. It happened like this. To settle a dispute between 

Brahma and Vishnu over who was the greater, the Lord appeared before them in 
a giant column of fire and challenged them to seek its top or bottom. Brahma 
assumed the form of a swan and flew high up into the sky, while Vishnu took the 
form of a boar and dug deep down into the earth. Try as they might, however, 
neither could accomplish the task. Humbled, Brahma and Vishnu recognised 
Lord Siva’s greatness and knelt down before him: “Lord, on account of our ego 
we have foolishly forgotten the Supreme Self. The earth longs for your blessing; 
please remain with us. Take form in the land of Bharatha so that gods and men 
may forever have the benefit of your darshan and thereby be set free!” 

Other devas heard Brahma and Vishnu’s pleas and joined in. It so happened that 
Lord Siva had recently felt anguished by the suffering of creation and yearned to 
show them the way to perfection. So he devised a plan to satisfy Brahma, Vishnu 
and the devas, as well as to serve all creation which struggled against the illusions of 
earthly life. From the column of fire, he would take form as a mighty, motionless

In the Beginning

mountain. At its feet, all could offer their prayers and devotion and receive his 
blessings. No sooner thought than done: in the heart of the Tamil land emerged 
a majestic peak. Devotees called it Arunachala — the Hill of Fire.

Now Brahma, Vishnu, Indra and the other devas were content. One by one 
they came to visit and worship this grandest mountain on earth. The Lord gave 
them spiritual instruction: “Meditate upon the effulgent lingam known as Aru-
nachala and thus be rid of your sins. While mountains like Kailasa and Meru are 
my places of abode, this Arunachala is my true form and here I abide eternally!” 

Sages and saints from great distances heard the good news and made haste 
to behold the Lord’s greatness in Tiruvannamalai. Even Parvati, Siva’s consort, 
came to do penance before this great lingam. Many were so blessed by the Lord’s 
presence that they were unable to leave. Centuries, indeed millennia, passed, 
with untold numbers of great souls, saints, sages and gods making yatra to win 
the Lord’s favour at Holy Annamalai.

Part I: Early Days on the Hill

Bhagavan Sri Ramana heard the name ‘Arunachala’ in his early childhood. 
In 1896, at the age of 16, he came in search of it. Some weeks earlier, he 
had had a transformative experience that left him changed forever. Hav-

ing fully realised the Self, he was now compelled by a mysterious inner call to 
leave his school, family and childhood home to take up residence at the feet of 
Holy Annamalai, never to leave its presence again.

Young Bhagavan moved up onto the Hill in 1899, staying at Virupaksha 
Cave. Situated beneath an immense rock on the eastern slope, this cave takes the 
shape of the sacred OM. Indeed, many say the sound of OM can be heard there. 

Virupaksha Cave is the heart of Arunachala’s glory and, over the centuries, 
great yogis and rishis have come to do sadhana within its hallowed walls. Like 
them, young Ramana was drawn by the cave’s magnetic influence.

Chapter 1 
A Sage’s Garden

In the early days, the young Swami lived virtually alone. But gradually people 
began to take notice of him. A mysterious and irresistible force seemed to 
overwhelm them as they saw what looked like eternity shining from his eyes. 

One by one they came, some for short visits, others for longer stays, and still 
others who would remain with him for the rest of their lives. 

But the silent power which attracted them also had its effect on other creatures 
living on the mountain. Many animals of the Hill such as squirrels, pigeons, 
wild cats and deer, as well as the numerous monkey tribes, were drawn to him. 
Of the latter were black-faced langurs, the powerful leaf-eating monkeys that 
moved in small bands on the hillside. Langurs, being shy, took their darshan at a 
distance. But the Bonnet macaque, a small rhesus-like monkey, was more sociable 
and not afraid to come for a closer look. As the opportunities for interaction 
were abundant, in time the young Swami got to know them well. Eventually he

was even able to understand the meaning of their calls, and they in turn began 
to understand his words and gestures. In time, a bond formed between them.

The scene in front of Virupaksha included sparrows and other birds who 
alighted on the branches of neem and mango trees, and peacocks who moved 
about pecking at the leftover grains from mealtime. Dogs lay curled up in the 
warmth of the morning sun. 

But even less attractive guests such as lizards, spiders and toads also came 
and were welcomed. Serpents regularly visited and once, to the horror of ob-
servers, a snake slithered right over the young Swami, who showed not even 
the least sign of concern. 

Another time, two scorpions climbed over him, one after the other, and again 
he remained calm as if nothing out of the ordinary were happening. 

On yet another occasion, a small frog, long and slender, sat at a distance of 
about 10 feet. Young Bhagavan gazed at it in silence as it stared back at him. 
Suddenly, without warning, and in a single bound, the critter leapt in the air 
and landed on his forehead. Residents gasped, fearing some harm to Swami’s 
eye. But the young Swami didn’t make any fuss, but his broad smile and slight 
chuckle suggested something like: “No need to worry. He’s just saying hello”.

Monkey Samadhi

However, Swami wasn’t always silent and serious. He often took advantage of 
opportunities for light-hearted interaction, especially with the monkeys 

at whom he loved to poke fun. 
The monkey clan came regularly just to be with him. In his presence, they

Bhagavan: Hey, hey, my friends! You’re sitting in samadhi, are you? Well done.

The Hill Tribe Comes  

to the Rescue

 

Brahmana Swami’s fellowship with animals often had unexpected benefits. 
Ashramites witnessed numerous instances of animal generosity, especially 
among monkeys. Like Hanuman who flew to the golden-peaked Hima-

van to retrieve the herb with which to heal Lakshmana’s wounds, so too the 
monkeys of Arunachala Mountain also tried to be of service whenever possible. 

One warm day, the sage set out with a number of devotees to do giri pradak-
shina, the time-honoured circuit of the Hill. The distance is some fourteen kilo-
metres. For a leisurely circuit, devotees often needed several hours or even a full 
day, depending on the season. As devotees walked along, one of them noticed a 
troop of monkeys trailing them…

Devotee: Hey, look! The monkeys are coming around the Hill with us.
Ashramite: We take the road, they take the branches.
Devotee: What do you think they’re up to?

Devotee: At least they have the shade of the trees to keep them cool!

Around one o’clock, tired and hungry for having missed their midday meal, 
the devotees drew near Pachaiamman Temple, the ancient Devi shrine on the

Ashramite: It’s amazing. Even without Bhagavan saying anything, they under-

Chapter 3
The Leaseholder and  
an Imaginary Curse

In rural Tamil Nadu, when villagers see a monkey passing through, they will 
prostrate before it, honouring it as an incarnation of Hanuman. But among 
the citizens of Tiruvannamalai, the hill monkeys neither enjoyed the rever-

ence of village India nor the affection shown them on the Hill by Bhagavan. 
They were mischievous and made regular raids on the township. On their vis-
its, they were notorious for wrecking freshly-washed clothes hanging out to dry or 
pilfering vegetables and fruit from street vendors. They were also on hand to harass 
pilgrims carrying fruit offerings at temples and shrines. So their popularity was not 
without limits.

In 1908, an epidemic broke out and gradually found its way to Tiruvan-
namalai. Because of Virupaksha’s proximity to the township, Sri Ramana was 
forced to leave the cave. His animal friends accompanied him, as did Ganapati 
Muni, the great Sanskrit scholar-poet, and other devotees and Ashram residents. 
They shifted to the more remote Pachaiamman Temple. One of the places where 
Parashakti came to do her penance, this holy site had been home to Bhagavan 
for six months during the epidemic of 1905 and now again, from January to

Part II: The Newcomer

In 1916, Bhagavan’s mother Alagammal came to live at Virupaksha. 
Bhagavan’s younger brother, having recently lost his wife and left with a 
little son, also joined them. Around this time, a devotee named Kandas-

wami began construction of a small ashram in a hollow just up the Hill from 
Virupaksha. Skandasramam, as it came to be called, was where Bhagavan had 
earlier discovered a perennial spring, and would serve as his home for the next 
six years. Joining him at the new location was an increasing number of devotees 
and, of course, Bhagavan’s monkey companions.

Chapter 4

Saving an Exiled 
 Monkey Prince

According to legend, a monkey-king once risked his life helping his subjects 
flee imminent danger. In order for them to cross the hazardous waters 
 of a fast-moving river, the monkey-king, suspended between branches, 

made himself into a bridge over which they could pass. In the process, however, 
he became exhausted and fell into the water. The human-king of the land was 
observing all this from nearby and took pity on him. Moved by the monkey-
king’s brave self-sacrifice, he came to his aid in a small rowboat. The monkey-
king was thankful to have been spared certain death, but the human-king was 
also grateful. Having sensed in the monkey’s selfless actions a noble spirit, the 
monkey had demonstrated to him the proper duty of a king — to value his 
subjects more than his own life. 

33



The Newcomer

Sometimes legend becomes reality. In the days just prior to moving from 
Virupaksha up to Skandasramam, the big leader of a monkey troop attacked 
a young monkey, causing him to fall from a high tree. When the little one fell 
and was badly injured, the leader was content to abandon him to his fate, and 
so had the rest of the tribe leave without him. Barely conscious, the little one 
limped and crawled to Bhagavan. He was slowly nursed back to health, with 
Bhagavan tenderly bandaging his wounded leg and hand-feeding him each day. 
His injuries soon healed, but the young monkey was left with a limp. 

While Bhagavan and Mother were taking care of this latest newcomer to the 
Ashram, some of the residents overheard them talking...

Part III: War and Peace  
at Skandasramam 

Skandasramam was the centre of life on the Hill. With its regular supply of 
sweet water, it had long been a gathering point. But Bhagavan’s presence 
significantly increased the attraction. All manner of creature came seeking 

refreshment, both from the reservoir of fresh water, as well as the well-spring of 
grace that was Bhagavan Ramana. Like the garden at Virupaksha, Skandasramam 
provided a place of refuge, sustenance and serenity. Towering above was a giant 
mango tree which supplied fruit and shade for all. However, although a place of 
peace, it was also the site of occasional disputes. Competing with one another for 
water and mangoes, or simply not wanting others around, disagreements arose 
between the various animal guests and, at times, even turned violent.

Bhagavan would often send him away on some pretext or other in order to al-
low them to roam about freely. Ever their sanctuary, Bhagavan was an oasis of 
benediction they could count on at any time.

A Timely Warning
Another time, a devotee was reading the newspaper to Bhagavan in the hall. 
There was an article in it discussing a team of veterinarians from Madras who 
were coming to collect monkeys for laboratory research. Upon hearing the news, 
Bhagavan turned to the clan gathered at the window and warned them of the 
approaching danger, advising them to go and hide for three days. Evidently they 
understood his words, as not one was seen for several days thereafter.

Taking Refuge in Bhagavan

Reverance For All

Bhagavan gave human devotees a potent sadhana — to practice compassion 
and empathy for all creatures. He spoke about how one could discover the 

intelligence in animals if one was patient enough to get to know them. When 
asked directly about this, Bhagavan said that animals are endowed with the same 
atman, the same divinity, that dwells in human beings, and are as eligible to 
finish their karma and attain liberation in this lifetime. Even Lakshmi, a cow, 
was said by Bhagavan to have been liberated at the time of her death in 1948. 

Bhagavan spoke on behalf of his animal friends and demanded the same 
rights for them as were enjoyed by his human devotees. A number of times, he 
lamented the fact that at Ramanasramam he was indoors where they didn’t have 
free access to him. He drove home the idea that no one, regardless of what “skin 
they might be wearing”, should be deprived of his darshan. Slowly, his disciples 
began to understand that Bhagavan’s animal friends were also his devotees and 
as dear to him as anyone else. 

One day when the monkey troop made mischief in the hall, Krishnaswami, 
Bhagavan’s personal attendant, chased them away, shouting all the while. When 
he began beating them, Bhagavan was beside himself with grief. In a pained tone 
that conveyed his empathy, Bhagavan stunned his attendant and all the others 
gathered in the hall when he said...

Bhagavan: It is not the monkeys that receive your beatings. It is I — the suf-
fering is mine!

Chapter 11 
Festivals and Fruit 

Year by year, the numbers at Sri Ramanasramam steadily increased. 
Gradually, intimate access to Bhagavan was reduced, especially for the 
monkeys. However, as visitors came in greater numbers so did their fruit 

offerings, which Bhagavan was always keen to share with his four-legged allies. 
Streams of devotees came for festivals. On such occasions, Bhagavan saw to it 

that Nondi and the company from the Hill were included in the celebrations. 
On Rama Navami, Lord Rama’s birthday, he would say, “This is their day” and 
would organize a special monkey banquet. Likewise, on Bhagavan’s birthday and 
on Mahapuja Day, the anniversary of his mother’s Mahasamadhi, he would see 
that food was set aside and taken to Palakotthu, the sadhu colony adjacent to the 
Ashram. Bhagavan would sit there and personally hand over food to each of his 
monkey friends. After having their fill, they would show their gratitude by cry-
ing out in one voice. Such moments gave Bhagavan great joy.

Independence Day

Bhagavan’s human disciples were often not as enthusiastic about his monkey 
friends as their Master was. This was especially true for the darshan hall 

attendants who, having to maintain order and protect visitors bearing offerings, 
often clashed with monkey visitors. Festival celebrations were no exception.

On Independence Day, Bhagavan’s attendant Krishnaswami was putting up 
the national flag. He was in high spirits. He was using a long bamboo pole to 
hoist the banner that would mark the day’s events. Devotees had gathered and 
were looking on, when out of nowhere a monkey appeared. For no obvious 
reason, Krishnaswami began to chase after the poor animal. He began to shout 
at him and then, in broad sweeping strokes, to beat wildly at him with the very 
pole that bore the nation’s flag at its end. The irony of the situation was not lost 
on Bhagavan who, watching from a distance, called out...

Bhagavan: Oh, Krishnaswami, today is Independence Day! Moreover, until 
recently, this place was the kingdom of the monkeys. However, when we en-
tered the picture, out of their good sense, they withdrew to the Hill. Today this 
monkey has come to participate in our Independence Day celebrations out of 
joy for the event. But you are, so unreasonably, driving him away with the very 
centrepiece of the day’s celebration. Has it occurred to you, dear fellow, how 
silly that looks?

Chapter 12
Crossing Enemy Lines

Nondi grew in wisdom over the years and, by Bhagavan’s grace, earned 
the respect of the monkeys on the Hill. He brought prosperity to his 
clan through his intelligence, strength of character and courage in bat-

tle. Yet perhaps his greatest gift to the tribe was the special place he held in Bhaga-
van’s heart, for it was through Nondi that Bhagavan’s blessings came to them. 

Despite this, some of the ministers loyal to the former king wanted their old 
positions back, and the power and influence it gave them. Nondi knew about 
their scheming, but decided to leave everything to destiny. Eventually, the trou-
blemakers succeeded and he was forced out of his tribe, although he didn’t seem 
to mind too much. Many remained loyal to Nondi and left with him, including 
his most senior minister, friend and chief commander, as well as his three queens 
and children. 

But now, since the strength and size of their group was greatly reduced, they 
had to be very careful when visiting Bhagavan, as the territory between Skan-
dasramam and Ramanasramam was ruled by unfriendly tribes.

Crossing Enemy Lines

Perilous Journeys

Early one morning, Kunjuswami observed Bhagavan sitting at the rear of the 
Ashram talking with a mother monkey and her baby. She seemed to be 

complaining to Bhagavan for having left Skandasramam and moving down to 
the foot of the mountain. Now, she had to risk travelling through enemy terri-
tory to come for Bhagavan’s darshan...

Bhagavan: (Listening carefully to her story) Amma, I’m sorry! There was nothing 
I could do. We had to come here.

On yet another occasion, Bhagavan and Kunjuswami were sitting on a bench 
resting from pradakshina, when they suddenly heard screeches from a distant 
banyan tree. With great urgency, Bhagavan rushed over to a group of monkeys 
sheltering in the branches, among them an old fellow holding a baby. As Bhaga-
van listened to the old monkey’s chattering, his face fell...

Bhagavan: Grandpa! What ill fortune has befallen you? At this advanced age 
you have to care for this young one? I’m sorry. But look after him with care. It’s 
your punya.

(Kunjuswami, looking perplexed, is unable to follow any of this.)

Kunjuswami: What’s going on?
Bhagavan: Do you see his plight? The mother of this baby died recently. Ac-
cording to custom, the oldest of the group must look after the orphaned. Now, 
the responsibility of bringing up this baby rests on our grandpa.

(Bhagavan looks up empathetically at the old fellow, who mutters plaintively...)

Kunjuswami: What’s he saying, Bhagavan?

(Bhagavan pauses)

Bhagavan: They’ve come to us very cautiously, as this is not their territory. 
When we were at Skandasramam, this same grandpa and his clan used to visit us

One afternoon, instead of his customary walk, Bhagavan went up to

Meanwhile, a band of monkeys approached the hall. A number of them remained

their fear of people and stepped inside. Discovering only an empty couch, the 
whole clan began to jump and scream as if they had a sense of things to come.

King Nondi’s Final Battle

Meanwhile, back on the Hill, competition between tribes for food and 
territory increased. Nondi’s band was now much more vulnerable to 

attack because of its smaller size. Battles were frequent, but the clan survived 
thanks to the daring and determination of Nondi and his closest minister. Be-
tween the two of them, they could hold off almost any challenge. 

One day, however, a large clan mounted an offensive in which Nondi’s faith-
ful companion was so badly wounded that he could not continue fighting. This 
left Nondi to repel the attack on his own. The opposing warriors — four agile 
males — were vicious in their assault and inflicted critical wounds on the lone 
defender. Unflinching, Nondi bravely stood his ground and was prepared to fight 
to the end. Feigning a strength that was quickly fading, he kept at it until the 
four finally began to get tired. They eventually lost the will to continue fighting 
and retreated into the trees with their supporters. 

Although victorious, Nondi was badly wounded and had lost a lot of blood. 
In the hours and days that followed, the energy continued to slip away from his 
shredded body. At the end of the third day, Nondi’s family and subjects watched 
as their king finally commended his spirit to Arunachala. Howls of anguish 
echoed from the hillside, breaking the night-time silence of the otherwise sleepy 
town of Tiruvannamalai below. 

The following morning, Nondi’s tribe longed to bring the corpse of their 
fallen leader to his Master and friend, Bhagavan Sri Ramana, for his final bless-
ing. However, the clan’s vulnerable condition and severe recent losses left them 
in no position to risk the journey through enemy lines. They performed the last 
rites on the battlefield itself, and Nondi’s mortal remains were returned to the 
five elements on the slopes of Holy Annamalai.

Epilogue: Last Days
Peace eventually returned to the Hill. Nondi’s trusted friend 

 and loyal comrade recovered from his battle wounds 
and took on the responsibility of protecting and lead-

ing the tribe. In time, the population of the clan 
grew and became a force once more. Over the 

years, Nondi’s children, grandchildren, nephews 
and nieces all came to visit Bhagavan. Some 
went to the window beside his sofa in the hall 
and peeped through the bars. Mothers with 
little ones clinging to their undersides came 
to present them to him. Others merely sat by 
and gazed at him. Bhagavan talked with them 
freely, listened to their troubles, and consoled 
them with fruit and words of advice.

At the end of 1948, doctors discovered a 
peanut-sized growth on Bhagavan’s left elbow. 
By the spring of 1949, it had grown into a 
large tumour. By and by, the doctors realised 
that there was not much they could do. 

The casualness with which Bhagavan watched 
the events of his earthly life unfold never ceased 
to astonish those who witnessed it. Others around 
him, however, were not so detached. 

One morning, some devotees sitting behind 
the stone sofa in the New Hall were sobbing in 

anguish about the malignant growth on Bhagavan’s 
arm, afraid that he might not be with them for much 

longer. Bhagavan responded gently, saying: “Where can 
I go? Where is it possible for me to go?” 

90



Last Days

Bhagavan’s energy gradually depleted. Nevertheless, he kept to his normal 
routine, taking his morning bath an hour before sunrise and sitting for darshan 
at fixed hours. He went through Ashram correspondence and supervised pub-
lications. Everything received his careful attention despite his declining health. 
He continued to welcome everyone and, as always, had a smile for each, even 
on his very last day. When the end was only a few hours away, two peacocks sat 
trumpeting from a nearby tree. Even then, Bhagavan’s only concern was whether 
they and his other animal friends had been given their usual food. 

Trailing Splendour

That evening, a Friday, the first day of the Tamil New Year and Pradosham, the 
ever-auspicious thirteenth lunar day, Nondi’s guru and friend left his body 

before a grieving gathering of devotees. At that instant, thousands witnessed a 
spectacular sight — a bright meteor traversed the Tiruvannamalai night-sky and 
made a triumphal exit beyond Annamalai’s summit. The following day, news-
papers in Chennai reported eyewitness accounts of the event from all over the 
state. At the time, not all knew that they were being blessed with the Maharshi’s 
last earthly darshan. But some devotees, even as far away as Nagpur and Pune, 
intuited it immediately, recognising this as the sage’s parting benediction. 

91



Now and Forever

Lord Siva came and took earthly form as Arunachala, and Lord Arunachala in 
 turn took human form as Bhagavan Sri Ramana. But devotees are quite 

right in saying that Bhagavan never went anywhere in 1950. Whether walking 
around the Hill or sitting in the hall at Sri Ramanasramam, Bhagavan’s presence 
is there still to greet the sincere pilgrim-seeker. 

As for Nondi’s progeny, they thrived, as did the human community in 
Tiruvannamalai. The monkey clan fanned out and settled on various parts 
of the mountain, as well as at Arunachala Temple and other shrines. Today, 
Nondi’s descendants may not recall his glory and the special place he had in 
the Master’s heart, but they still receive Bhagavan’s blessings in abundance and 
come for regular visits to the Ashram that bears his name. There, even after so 
many years, Bhagavan’s teaching about respect for animals is still cherished, and 
Nondi’s descendants and other animals are welcomed and provided for — much 
as they were in the days of old.

still at Skandasramam, and stayed until the end except for brief 
absences. Known for his photographic memory and enthusiasm

Glossary

prasad Sacramental offerings in the form of edibles. After having been 
offered to the deity or guru, they are distributed to devotees.

puja Ritual worship of deity employing a prescribed litany of prayers 
and mantras followed by arati or waving of the sacred flame.

Punarvasu One of the 27 stars or lunar mansions of the moon. At the time 
of Sri Ramana’s birth, the moon was in this division between 20 
degrees Gemini and 3.33 degrees of Cancer.

punya Merit. Good deeds. 
sadhaka Spiritual seeker.
sadhana Spiritual practice.
samadhi Place of burial of saint; deep absorption as in a meditative state.
sannyasin Renunciate monk.
siddha purushas Holy beings or saintly spirits.
Skandasramam Built above Virupaksha in 1916 by Kandaswami. Work began 

as early as 1902. Accounts testify to Kandaswami’s unswerving 
dedication in carving out a space on the hillside for the ashram 
that bears his name.

Sona Tirtham Near the Ganesh temple where Sri Bhagavan used to stop for rest 
when doing giri pradakshina.

Swami Title of respect and generally used for all renunciates.
tamarind Tree which gives small pods with sour taste used in cooking. 

Traditionally it is inauspicious to have tamarind trees on one’s 
property, though there is a huge demand for their fruit because of 
its value in cooking. 

tapas Penance or sacrifices made by a spiritual aspirant.
tirtham Temple tank for ritual bathing or holy water from puja.
upadhis Spiritual teaching.
Valli the Deer Given as a youngster to Bhagavan. After hunters injured her, 

Bhagavan and ashramites attempted to nurse her but without 
success. She breathed her last in Bhagavan’s lap. 

vibhuti Sacred ash used in ceremonies and rituals, believed to purify both 
body and spirit from defilements and to have healing powers. 

Virupaksha The cave where the poet-saint of the Vira Saiva sect credited with 
	 Cave authorship of Cenna Basava Purana lived and did tapas. Home to 

Sri Bhagavan for seventeen years from 1899-1916.
